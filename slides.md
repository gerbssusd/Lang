# Where language lives in a narrative

Ribó separates the **story**, "what the narrator tells the narratee," from the **discourse**, "how the narrative is conveyed from the implied author to the implied reader" <Cite>Ribó, ch. 1</Cite>.

<div class="tint mt-4">
<h3>The key move of chapter 6</h3>
<p class="!mb-0">Discourse has a content (the story) <em>and</em> a form. "The form of discourse is what we generally call its style" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.1</Cite>.</p>
</div>

<div class="mt-4 small muted">So when we analyze language, we are analyzing the outer arrow of this diagram: the implied author's choices, sentence by sentence.</div>

<!--
Let's begin with the model that organizes Ribó's entire book. A narrative, he explains, has two levels. The story is what the narrator tells the narratee: the events, the characters, the environments. The discourse is how the narrative is conveyed, from an implied author to an implied reader. In chapter six, Ribó adds a crucial refinement. Discourse has a content, which is the story, but it also has a form. And the form of discourse is what we generally call its style. That is the level we are studying today. When we look closely at a metaphor or the rhythm of a sentence, we are looking at the outer arrow of this diagram, at the choices of the implied author, which we can study from the text alone, whatever we know about the real author.
-->

# Nothing in the storyworld exists except in words

<Passage cite="Ribó, sec. 6.2" size="sm">
The existents of the storyworld (events, environments, and characters) only exist insofar as narrative discourse succeeds in representing them in the imagination of the reader.
</Passage>

<div class="grid grid-cols-3 gap-3 mt-4 small">
<div class="card"><h3>Events</h3>emplotted in ch. 2: order, conflict, suspense, surprise</div>
<div class="card"><h3>Environments</h3>arranged as setting in ch. 3: topography and atmosphere</div>
<div class="card"><h3>Characters</h3>individuated in ch. 4: speech, action, looks, dialogue</div>
</div>

<p class="small muted mt-4">Language is therefore "burdened, not only with conveying meaning, but also with recreating the whole context where meaning can emerge" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.2</Cite>.</p>

<!--
Chapters two through four of Ribó's book describe the three kinds of existents that populate a storyworld: events, which the narrator arranges into a plot; environments, which become setting; and characters. This triangle, from chapter three, shows how they depend on each other. Chapter six makes a striking claim about all three. These existents only exist insofar as narrative discourse succeeds in representing them in the imagination of the reader. In conversation, speakers share a context. In fiction, the reader has no direct access to the storyworld, so language carries a double burden. It must convey meaning, and it must also recreate the whole context in which meaning can emerge. Keep that double burden in mind. A diamond mountain, a papered nursery, and a dark plantation walk exist only as sentences.
-->

# Five instruments for reading language

<div class="grid grid-cols-5 gap-3 mt-2">
<div class="card"><div class="big-num">6.1</div><h3 class="!mt-2">Style</h3><p class="small">"A characteristic set of linguistic features associated with a text," attributable to the implied author.</p></div>
<div class="card"><div class="big-num">6.2</div><h3 class="!mt-2">Foregrounding</h3><p class="small">Features that deviate from the "norm" of everyday language and draw attention to themselves.</p></div>
<div class="card"><div class="big-num">6.3</div><h3 class="!mt-2">Figures</h3><p class="small">Metaphor, simile, personification, metonymy, synecdoche, hyperbole, oxymoron.</p></div>
<div class="card"><div class="big-num">6.4</div><h3 class="!mt-2">Symbol</h3><p class="small">An existent that "represents something else by virtue of an arbitrary association"; sustained, it becomes allegory.</p></div>
<div class="card"><div class="big-num">6.5</div><h3 class="!mt-2">Translation</h3><p class="small">Every translation is an interpretation; style resists it most where foregrounding is highest.</p></div>
</div>

<p class="small muted mt-5">All definitions: <Cite>Ribó, secs. 6.1–6.5</Cite>. Ribó draws on rhetoric and on modern <em>stylistics</em>, the application of linguistics to literary texts.</p>

<!--
Chapter six gives us five instruments, and we will use all of them. The first is style, which Ribó defines as a characteristic set of linguistic features associated with a text. The second is foregrounding: the features of a text that deviate from ordinary language and so come forward, into the reader's attention. The third is figurative language, the classic figures of speech such as metaphor, simile, and personification. The fourth is symbolism. A symbol is anything that represents something else by virtue of an arbitrary association, and when symbolism is sustained across a whole narrative, the story becomes an allegory. And the fifth is translation, which reminds us that style is exactly what is hardest to carry from one language into another. Ribó draws these ideas from two traditions: ancient rhetoric, and the modern discipline of stylistics.
-->

# Style is a sum of choices

Style "generally results from multiple and complex decisions about rhythm, phonological patterns, syntactic structure, lexical choice, collocation, paragraph organisation" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.2</Cite>.

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-3 mt-3 small">
<div class="card"><h3>Attributed to…</h3>the implied author (from the text alone), the real author, a genre, a period, or a culture.</div>
<div class="card"><h3>Our three styles</h3><span class="fitz-c font-bold">lavish</span> · <span class="gilman-c font-bold">fractured</span> · <span class="chopin-c font-bold">restrained</span></div>
</div>

<Passage cite="Flaubert qtd. in Ribó, sec. 6.1" size="sm" class="mt-4">
Human language is like a cracked pot on which we tap crude melodies to make bears dance, while we long to melt the stars.
</Passage>

<!--
What is style, concretely? Ribó describes it as the result of many decisions, about rhythm, sound patterns, sentence structure, word choice, which words keep company with which, and how paragraphs are organized. We can attribute a style to the implied author of a single text, to a real author across several works, or even to a genre or a period. The image here is Flaubert's first draft of A Simple Heart, crowded with crossings-out. Ribó uses it to show how consciously some writers labor over every phrase. Flaubert's narrator in Madame Bovary calls human language a cracked pot on which we tap crude melodies to make bears dance, while we long to melt the stars. Hold on to those stars. They will return in all three of our stories. For now, let me give you three one-word labels for our authors' styles. Fitzgerald's is lavish. Gilman's is fractured. Chopin's is restrained.
-->

# Background and foreground

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-5 mt-2">
<div class="card">
<h3 class="!text-[var(--c-muted)]">The "norm"</h3>
<p class="font-serif text-[1.2rem] !leading-snug">The silence was interrupted by the buzzing of bees around the plants.</p>
<p class="small muted">Ribó's plain paraphrase: economical, efficient, transparent <Cite>sec. 6.2</Cite>.</p>
</div>
<div class="card">
<h3>Foregrounded (Wilde, <em>Dorian Gray</em>)</h3>
<p class="font-serif italic text-[1.12rem] !leading-snug">The sullen murmur of the bees shouldering their way through the long unmown grass … seemed to make the stillness more oppressive.</p>
<p class="small muted">Qtd. in Ribó, sec. 6.2. Adjectives, personification, sound.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div v-click class="tint mt-5 grid grid-cols-[auto_1fr] gap-4 items-center">
<div class="i-ph-warning-circle text-3xl text-[var(--accent)]" />
<p class="!m-0 small"><strong>Ribó's caution:</strong> there is no "style-free norm." Even the plain sentence alliterates (<em>buzzing of bees</em>). Foregrounding is a matter of <strong>degree</strong>, and it does work: it builds descriptions, characters, and atmosphere.</p>
</div>

<!--
The second instrument is foregrounding. Ribó demonstrates it with bees. Here, on the left, is a sentence written to convey its meaning as economically as possible: the silence was interrupted by the buzzing of bees around the plants. On the right is Oscar Wilde's version, in The Picture of Dorian Gray, where the bees are sullen, they shoulder their way through unmown grass, and the stillness itself becomes oppressive. The language comes forward. We notice it, and it shapes our experience of the setting.
[click]
But Ribó adds an important caution. There is no truly style-free norm. Even his plain sentence alliterates, in the buzzing of bees. So foregrounding is a matter of degree, and it is functional. It is how prose fiction builds atmosphere, describes characters, and recreates a storyworld. As we will see, Fitzgerald foregrounds almost everything, Gilman foregrounds her narrator's mind, and Chopin keeps language in the background until the moment it matters.
-->

<div class="tint small !p-3"><h3>Beyond the list</h3><strong>Dead</strong> tropes ("fallen in love"), <strong>clichés</strong> ("teeth like pearls"), and <strong>conceits</strong>, figures too far-fetched to work.</div>
</div>

<p class="small muted mt-3">Examples as quoted in <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.3</Cite>. Figurative language is "the use of language in ways that deviate from the literal meaning of words and sentences."</p>

<!--
The third instrument is the figure of speech. Ribó defines figurative language as the use of language in ways that deviate from the literal meaning of words and sentences, and he lists seven common figures. A metaphor equates a tenor with a vehicle. His example comes from Kate Chopin's The Storm: her mouth was a fountain of delight. We will come back to that sentence when we reach Chopin. A simile makes the comparison explicit with like or as. Personification gives human traits to something nonhuman. Metonymy substitutes a thing for something contiguous to it, and synecdoche substitutes a part for the whole, or the whole for a part. Hyperbole exaggerates, and oxymoron joins apparent contradictions. Ribó also warns us about dead tropes, which have become ordinary speech, clichés, which have lost their force, and conceits, which are too far-fetched to work. Each of our authors has a signature figure. For Fitzgerald it is hyperbole, for Gilman it is personification, and for Chopin, as we will see, it is metonymy.
-->

# When an existent stands for something else

<div class="flex flex-col gap-3">
<div class="card small"><h3>Symbol</h3>"Anything that represents something else by virtue of an arbitrary association"; any existent can become one.</div>
<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-3 small">
<div class="card"><h3>Internal</h3>refers to other existents of the story (Harry's scar).</div>
<div class="card"><h3>External</h3>refers to discourse or the reader's world (the name <em>Dumbledore</em>).</div>
</div>
<div class="tint small"><h3>Allegory · Allegoresis</h3>Allegory, "to speak of something else," is symbolism sustained across a whole story; <strong>allegoresis</strong> is "the act of reading any story as allegory" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.4</Cite>. Compare ch. 3's <strong>symbolic setting</strong> <Cite>sec. 3.3</Cite>.</div>
</div>

<!--
The fourth instrument is symbolism. For Ribó, a symbol is anything that represents something else by virtue of an arbitrary association, and in a narrative any existent can become one: an event, an environment, or a character. He distinguishes internal symbols, which point to other things inside the storyworld, from external symbols, which point outward, to the level of discourse or to the reader's own world, as when a character's name carries a meaning in Latin or Old English. When symbolism structures an entire story, we have an allegory, from the Greek phrase meaning to speak of something else. The illustration here is Orwell's Animal Farm, Ribó's example of a political allegory. And there is a related idea we will need for Gilman: allegoresis, the act of reading a story as allegory, whether or not the author intended it. Remember too that chapter three describes symbolic setting, where an environment itself carries meaning.
-->

# A sunset that is already a wound

<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 153" size="md">
The Montana sunset lay between two mountains like <mark>a gigantic bruise</mark> from which <mark>dark arteries</mark> spread themselves over <mark>a poisoned sky</mark>.
</Passage>

<div class="grid grid-cols-3 gap-3 mt-4">
<Trope name="Simile" tenor="sunset" vehicle="bruise">"like a gigantic bruise"</Trope>
<Trope name="Metaphor" tenor="clouds" vehicle="arteries">"dark arteries"</Trope>
<Trope name="Personification" tenor="sky" vehicle="a poisoned body">"a poisoned sky"</Trope>
</div>

<p class="small mt-4">Ribó's <strong>symbolic setting</strong> <Cite>sec. 3.3</Cite>: before we see the diamond, the landscape is already <em>injured</em>. The village of Fish "sucked a lean milk from the almost literally bare rock" <Cite>Fitzgerald 153</Cite>. Foregrounding here builds <strong>atmosphere</strong>, and foreshadows <Cite>Ribó, secs. 3.2, 2.5</Cite>.</p>

<!--
Watch how Fitzgerald opens his second chapter. The Montana sunset lay between two mountains like a gigantic bruise, from which dark arteries spread themselves over a poisoned sky. Three figures work in a single sentence. The simile compares the sunset to a bruise. The metaphor turns the streaks of cloud into arteries. And the poisoned sky personifies the landscape as a wounded body. Ribó would call this a foregrounded description, and he reminds us, in chapter three, that setting can be symbolic, calling attention to other elements of the story such as plot or theme. Here the landscape is injured before we ever see the diamond. The nearby village of Fish, whose twelve men suck a lean milk from bare rock, is another image of poverty drawing on stone. So this lush language does atmospheric work, and it foreshadows. Beauty, in this story, is always sitting on top of violence.
-->

# Names that speak of something else

<div class="grid grid-cols-3 gap-3 mt-1">
<div class="card"><h3>Hades</h3><p class="small">John's hometown, where "the earnest worship of and respect for riches" is "the first article of its creed" <Cite>Fitzgerald 156</Cite>. Every host's joke: "Pretty hot down there?" <Cite>151–52</Cite>.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>St. Midas's</h3><p class="small">The school named for the king whose touch turned everything, even food, to gold <Cite>151</Cite>.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>Washington</h3><p class="small">"A direct descendant of George Washington, and Lord Baltimore" <Cite>163</Cite>: the nation's founding name placed on a secret, unsurveyed kingdom "where the United States ends" <Cite>157</Cite>.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>Fish</h3><p class="small">Twelve "sombre and inexplicable souls" watching a train as a "congregation": a parody of the twelve apostles <Cite>153–54</Cite>.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>The Ritz</h3><p class="small">The measuring stick of the title: the diamond's size is expressed in the unit of a luxury hotel <Cite>153</Cite>.</p></div>
<div class="tint"><h3>Ribó's term</h3><p class="small !mb-0"><strong>External symbols</strong> point beyond the storyworld to the reader's lifeworld (myth, scripture, national history) <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.4</Cite>.</p></div>
</div>

<!--
Fitzgerald also works through names, which Ribó would call external symbols, because their meaning comes from outside the storyworld, from the reader's own culture. John comes from Hades, where, the narrator says, the worship of riches is the first article of its creed. Every rich father he meets makes the same joke: pretty hot down there? He attends the school of Saint Midas, named for the king whose golden touch turned even his food to gold. His host is a Washington, descended from George Washington himself, who rules a patch of land where, in Percy's words, the United States ends. The twelve men of Fish gather like a congregation of apostles, but without any faith at all. And the diamond is measured in the unit of a luxury hotel. These names turn a fantasy into a satire of an American religion of money, with its own hell, its own saint, and its own founding father.
-->

# The master trope is exaggeration

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.15fr_1fr] gap-5">
<div class="flex flex-col gap-3">
<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 153" size="sm">My father has a diamond <mark>bigger than the Ritz-Carlton Hotel</mark>.</Passage>
<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 161" size="sm">One diamond, <mark>one cubic mile without a flaw</mark>.</Passage>
<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 159" size="sm">a room that was like <mark>a platonic conception of the ultimate prison</mark> … lined with an unbroken mass of diamonds</Passage>
</div>
<div class="flex flex-col gap-3">
<Trope name="Hyperbole">The title's hyperbole is literal inside the storyworld: fantasy lets the exaggeration come true.</Trope>
<Trope name="Oxymoron">"A chaste and consistent selfishness ran like a pattern through their every idea" <Cite>178</Cite>.</Trope>
<div class="tint small">Luxury described as <strong>prison</strong>; selfishness described as <strong>virtue</strong>. The figures quietly judge what the characters admire <Cite>Ribó, secs. 6.3, 4.4</Cite>.</div>
</div>
</div>

<!--
If one figure dominates this story, it is hyperbole. Percy's boast, that his father has a diamond bigger than the Ritz-Carlton Hotel, sounds like a schoolboy exaggeration. Then, in the logic of the fantasy, it turns out to be literally true: one diamond, one cubic mile without a flaw. Fitzgerald lets the exaggeration become the storyworld. Now look at the figures he uses to describe the family's treasures. A room lined with diamonds is like a platonic conception of the ultimate prison. And the children's character is summed up in an oxymoron: a chaste and consistent selfishness ran like a pattern through their every idea. Chastity and consistency are virtues. Selfishness is not. In Ribó's terms from chapter four, the narrator is characterizing indirectly, through figures. The language admires, and in the same breath it judges. Luxury is a prison, and selfishness has been dressed up as a virtue.
-->

# Irony: the language of property

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-5">
<div class="flex flex-col gap-3">
<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 166" size="sm">But <mark>very few other murders</mark> stained these <mark>happy years of progress and expansion</mark>.</Passage>

<Passage cite="Fitzgerald 191–92" size="sm">

<div class="card"><h3>Allegorical climax</h3>The plot's peak (Freytag's apex) is a scene of pure symbolism: wealth attempting to purchase the universe itself <Cite>Ribó, secs. 2.4, 6.4</Cite>.</div>

<!--

# A style that interrupts itself

<Passage cite="Gilman 198" size="sm">
perhaps—(I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but <mark>this is dead paper</mark> and a great relief to my mind)—perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster.

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-3 mt-3 small">
<div class="card"><h3>Syntax</h3>Dashes, parentheses, exclamations, and one-line paragraphs; the refrain "what is one to do?" <Cite>198–99</Cite>.</div>
<div class="card"><h3>Narration</h3>First person, inward focalisation, simultaneous telling in diary entries <Cite>Ribó, secs. 5.2–5.3</Cite>.</div>
</div>

<div class="tint small mt-3">Like Gogol's madman, Ribó's example, she becomes an <strong>unreliable narrator</strong>: the implied author communicates "at the expense of the narrator" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 5.5</Cite>. Her style is evidence we read <em>past</em> her.</div>

<!--

# The pattern becomes bars, and the bars hold a woman

<div class="grid grid-cols-4 gap-3 mt-2">
<div v-click class="card"><div class="big-num">1</div><h3>Setting</h3><p class="small">A nursery with "windows … barred," "rings and things in the walls," a bed "nailed down" <Cite>Gilman 200, 205</Cite>.</p></div>

<div v-click class="card"><div class="big-num">3</div><h3>Symbol</h3><p class="small">"like a woman stooping down and creeping about behind that pattern" <Cite>207</Cite>; then "a great many women" <Cite>212</Cite>.</p></div>
<div v-click class="card"><div class="big-num">4</div><h3>Identity</h3><p class="small">"I wonder if they all come out of that wallpaper <strong>as I did</strong>?" <Cite>215</Cite> "I've got out at last" <Cite>216</Cite>.</p></div>
</div>

<div v-after class="tint mt-5 small"><strong>Allegoresis</strong> <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.4</Cite>: readers take the paper as an allegory of domestic and medical confinement. The simile's "like" disappears, and tenor and vehicle merge as the narrator merges with the woman she sees.</div>

<!--
Now we can trace how a figure grows into a symbol. The movement is Gilman's plot.
[click]
It begins with the setting, which Ribó would call symbolic setting. The nursery has barred windows, rings in the walls, and a bed that is nailed down.
[click]
Then a figure. By moonlight, the narrator writes, the outside pattern becomes bars.
[click]

[click]

Her desire, or her respectability? The irony is left unresolved. Remember that Ribó's example of metaphor was Chopin's own sentence from The Storm, her mouth was a fountain of delight. # What would a translator lose?

Translations "are always interpretations or rewritings of the original," and difficulty "tends to increase with the degree of foregrounding" <Cite>Ribó, sec. 6.5</Cite>.

Ribó's final section concerns translation. Every translation, he argues, is an interpretation or a rewriting, and the difficulty increases with the degree of foregrounding. So translation makes a useful thought experiment for locating style. How would you translate Hades, and the joke, pretty hot down there, so that a town name, a punchline, and a theology still fit into one word? How would you translate Gilman's pun on figures, meaning both numbers and figures of speech? Or her yellow smell? And how would you carry the social weight of the word respectable into a culture with different codes of propriety? Chopin's story is already multilingual, since Gaston speaks to his wife in French. Here is an exercise. Pick one sentence from any of the three stories and paraphrase it plainly, as Ribó did with the bees. Whatever disappears in your paraphrase is the style.
-->
